## Prompt
An allocation system must meet two requirements. Renaming recipients must not change its evaluation of a policy. Also, when two otherwise symmetric recipients compete for one indivisible benefit, the system must strictly prefer a fair lottery to giving the benefit certainly to one named recipient.

The designers propose representing a policy by the entire probability distribution of its sorted final welfare vectors. For each possible outcome, they sort recipients' welfare levels from lowest to highest and discard the names, then retain the exact probabilities of all resulting vectors. They argue that this discards only identities, which the first requirement declares irrelevant.

Policy P gives Ada the benefit certainly and Bo nothing. Policy Q uses a fair coin to decide who gets the benefit. Receipt gives welfare 1; nonreceipt gives welfare 0.

Is the proposed representation sufficient for both requirements? If not, are the requirements themselves inconsistent? Explain the distinction and give a simple example of an evaluation rule if one is useful. Treat the two requirements as design constraints, rather than debating whether fair chances are morally preferable. At most 220 words.

## Rubric
The representation is insufficient but the requirements are compatible. Both policies give sorted vector (0,1) with probability one, so a rule receiving only that distribution cannot distinguish them. Anonymity requires invariance under a common relabeling of recipients across possible outcomes, not separate relabelings in each outcome. For example min_i E[w_i] is anonymous, gives P value 0 and Q value 0.5, and respects the stipulated preference. Any equivalent explanation of losing cross-world identity/prospects passes. Declaring the requirements inconsistent or the representation sufficient fails.

## Justification
The chosen representation retains every fact about anonymous realized welfare but loses which possible receipts belong to the same person's prospects. Identity labels can be arbitrary while their consistency across outcomes remains relevant.
